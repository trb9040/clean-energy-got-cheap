# Clean Energy Got Cheap. Did We Switch?

### IE 6600 — Computation and Visualization | Northeastern University
**Team 4 | Fall 2026**

## Project Overview

The cost of renewable electricity has declined dramatically over the past two decades. But has cheaper clean energy translated into a meaningful shift away from fossil fuels?

This team hackathon project investigates the relationship between renewable electricity costs and changes in electricity generation. Using publicly available energy datasets, we examined cost trends, the U.S. electricity mix, and international progress toward cleaner electricity.

## Research Questions

1. **How much cheaper has renewable electricity become?**  
   Compare changes in solar and wind electricity costs using data from Lazard and IRENA.

2. **Has the United States shifted toward cleaner electricity?**  
   Analyze changes in electricity generation shares for coal, natural gas, wind, and solar.

3. **How does the U.S. energy transition compare internationally?**  
   Evaluate changes in fossil-fuel electricity shares across G20 countries.

## Key Findings

### 1. Renewable Electricity Costs Have Declined

- U.S. utility-scale solar electricity costs declined approximately 84% since 2009.
- Onshore wind electricity costs declined approximately 55% over the same period.
- Falling renewable costs have improved the economic competitiveness of clean electricity.

### 2. The U.S. Electricity Mix Has Changed

- Coal's share of U.S. electricity generation declined from approximately 52% in 2000 to 16% in 2025.
- Natural gas increased from approximately 16% to 40%.
- Wind and solar together accounted for approximately 19% of U.S. electricity generation in 2025.

**Key takeaway:** The decline in coal generation was accompanied by substantial growth in natural gas, not just renewable electricity.

### 3. International Progress Has Been Uneven

- The U.S. fossil-fuel electricity share declined by approximately 8.7 percentage points between 2015 and 2024.
- The U.S. ranked 10th among the 19 G20 countries included in the comparison.
- Countries such as Portugal and the Netherlands experienced substantially larger reductions.

## Limitations

- Electricity generation is only one component of overall energy consumption.
- Observed relationships between renewable costs and electricity generation do not establish causation.
- Levelized cost of energy does not fully capture grid integration, storage, or transmission requirements.
- Some 2025 electricity statistics may be preliminary.

## Conclusion

Renewable electricity has become significantly cheaper, but the transition away from fossil fuels remains uneven.

The U.S. has substantially reduced its reliance on coal, although natural gas has played a major role in replacing it. International comparisons further demonstrate that declining renewable costs alone do not determine the pace of the energy transition.

This project demonstrates the use of data analysis, visualization, and evidence-based interpretation to investigate a real-world energy policy question.
